The moment it clicked

People don't use products the way we imagine they do.

In digital banking, a decision that seems obvious to a product team may not make the same sense to the person using the app.
A name can raise doubts. A feature designed to make a task easier can go unnoticed.

What happens in a person's mind when they interact with a product?

People interpret what they see, build expectations, and act on what they understand. And that interpretation doesn't always match what we designed.

At Interbank, I researched experiences around payments, savings, navigation, and digital sales, exploring how people understood interfaces, what they expected from them, and what influenced their behavior.

Discovering everything that happens behind an interaction is what connected me with UX Research: the chance to look beyond the obvious and question our own assumptions.

I'm interested in understanding how people make sense of what we design and how that knowledge can help us make better product decisions.

Research that put everyday operations first in the redesigned Interbank app Home

Duration
6 weeks
Role
UX Researcher
Team
2 product designers + 2 design researchers + 3 growth hackers
Scope
Home, navigation and product discovery
Tools
Figma · Maze · Qualtrics · Zoom · Claude

TL;DR

Interbank, a leading bank in Peru with 5M+ customers and 3M+ app users, wanted its app to become the main channel for everyday banking, ahead of other mobile banking apps. Usage data and primacy studies showed it wasn't users' first choice for payments and frequent operations. Together with another researcher, I planned and ran an iterative mixed-methods study to understand what people needed to find when they open the app, and how to reorganize the Home so everyday operations are easy to reach without making it harder to discover banking products. The redesigned Home reached production.

chose payments and operations among what they do most often (729 respondents)
65.5%
named payments and operations among the most important things to find on the Home (706 respondents)

Key indicators: Transactionality + Discovery + Engagement + Conversion

Solution · Key findings

Operations first, products one tap away, and an onboarding to guide the change

Order of operations by usage

People value quick access to their operations from the first view, in an order based on how often they use them.

First three on the Home

  1. Plin
  2. Transfers
  3. Card payment

Safety through hidden products

Finding their accounts in a second view contributed to a sense of security, especially in public spaces.

Support for the transition

Adding an onboarding improved success and reduced the time needed to find products.

Balance visible to operate

Needing to check their balance before operating helps people decide which operations to perform and for how much. Seeing it from the first screen of the flow lets them continue with confidence.

Impact on the business

The insights from this research directly shaped the product roadmap, leading to:

  • Redefinition of the Home's information architecture, especially the hierarchy between operations and products.
  • Selection of one Home proposal over the other, backed by quantitative and qualitative evidence.
  • Design adjustments to the chosen proposal based on frictions found in the qualitative study.
  • An onboarding as a key factor for the first release to production.

Problem

The Interbank app wasn't users' first choice for everyday operations

Usage data and primacy studies showed that Interbank's app wasn't the first option for its users to make payments and other frequent operations.

The Home at the time didn't prioritize users' main actions, such as payments and frequent operations.

Challenge

Understand what people needed to find when they enter the app, and how to reorganize the Home to ease access to usual operations without making it harder to discover banking products.

Scope

Home, navigation and product discovery.

Research impact

Research applied to design decisions for a Home that reached production.

Research

Eight steps of iterative research behind each product decision

I combined quantitative and qualitative methods, with an iterative analysis process that produced preliminary findings for quick design decisions.

  1. 01

    Exploration and benchmark

    Benchmark of banking and payment apps to find opportunities to improve access to everyday operations.

    → Decision: initial criteria for a Home oriented to everyday operations.

  2. 02

    Survey

    Qualtrics · 729 responses. What users do most in the app and what they expect on the Home.

    → Decision: focus proposals on giving operations the leading role.

  3. 03

    Usage metrics

    Frequency of use of each operation, to decide which ones deserve direct shortcuts.

    → Decision: priority and order of the Home shortcuts.

  4. 04

    First design proposals

    The design team explored different navigation structures based on the research so far.

    → Decision: two proposals to test, tabs and nav bar.

  5. 05

    Guerrilla testing

    Customers in bank branches explored both proposals to find comprehension problems and preferences.

    → Decision: the winning proposal was chosen.

  6. 06

    Moderated testing via Zoom

    Usability + interviews · 15 participants. Clarity, usefulness, ease of use and frictions of the new Home.

    → Decision: tabs confirmed, with adjustments to onboarding and balance visibility.

  7. 07

    Design iteration

    Findings translated into a clearer proposal: refined onboarding and balance visible before operating.

    → Decision: what still needed validation before production.

  8. 08

    Unmoderated validation: Maze

    Onboarding vs. no onboarding, to see if users could find the products tab.

    → Decision: onboarding prioritized for the first release.

01 · Exploration and benchmark

Objective: identify opportunities to improve access to everyday operations and the organization of the Home.

Method: benchmark of banking and payment apps, including GoPay, Nubank, BBVA, N26 and Revolut.

What we explored

  • How operations are presented from the main screen.
  • How access to frequent or favorite operations is made easy.
  • How offers and commercial opportunities are integrated into the experience.

Contribution: the benchmark helped identify relevant design patterns and set initial criteria to explore a Home more oriented to everyday operations.

02 · Quantitative research: user priorities

Objective: understand what customers did most often in the app and what they expected to find on the Home.

Method: quantitative survey built in Qualtrics and distributed in-app to active users of the banking app. Sample: 729 responses.

What I investigated

  • Operations done most often.
  • Content considered most important on the Home.
  • Relative priority of operations versus access to products.

What do you do most often in your banking app?

729 respondents

  • Make payments and operations81.5%
  • Make frequent operations50.5%
  • See my products10%

What matters most to you to have on your banking app's main screen?

706 respondents

  • Make payments and operations65.5%
  • Have frequent operations52.5%
  • See pre-approved product offers22%
  • See my products17.5%

Main results

  • 81.5% selected payments and operations among the activities they do most often.
  • 65.5% considered payments and operations among the most important things to find on the Home.

Contribution: the results supported the need to give everyday operations more visibility and provided a quantitative basis to guide the content hierarchy.

03 · Usage metrics: prioritizing operations

Objective: define which operations needed more visibility and in what order to present them on the Home.

Method: review of usage metrics for the app's operations.

What I analyzed

  • How often each operation is used.
  • Which operations should be prioritized as direct shortcuts on the Home.
  1. Plin to a phone number
  2. Pay services
  3. Transfer money
  4. Top up phone
  5. Transport top-up
  6. Currency exchange

Contribution: usage metrics complemented the survey results and served as criteria to prioritize and order the shortcuts in the first design proposals.

04 · First design proposals: hierarchy and navigation

The design team developed initial proposals based on what the research had shown, with different navigation structures to evaluate which one made it easier to reach everyday operations and to discover products.

05 · Qualitative exploration: guerrilla testing

Objective: contrast the first proposals with users and identify comprehension problems and opportunities for improvement.

Method: guerrilla tests with customers present in a bank branch.

What I explored

  • Understanding of the structure and organization of each Home proposal.
  • How easy it was to locate products and find the main operations.
  • Understanding of the interface elements and what they do.
  • Which proposal users preferred after exploring both.

2 of 3 customers preferred Tabs

Contribution: it made it possible to contrast both proposals and identify the one that was clearer and preferred by users, providing evidence for the next design iteration.

06 · Moderated qualitative evaluation

Main objective: evaluate the understanding, perception and ease of use of the new Home proposal, identifying frictions and opportunities to improve its structure and features.

Method: moderated sessions over Zoom, combining exploration tasks and interface evaluation with interviews.

Sample: 15 participants, including single-product users, multi-product users and merchants.

What I investigated

  • Understanding of how the Home is organized, and how easy it was to find operations and products through tabs.
  • Perception of the visual hierarchy and the usefulness of the shortcuts to everyday operations.
  • Interpretation of labels, icons and commercial elements, and the expectations they created.
  • Usefulness and clarity of the onboarding to find their way around the interface changes.

Main results

  • People valued having operations at first sight. The number of options felt adequate and the order of the first three was seen as consistent with how often they use them.
  • More privacy when checking products: finding products in a second view felt safer, especially when using the app in public spaces.
  • Needing to check their balance before operating helps them decide which operations to perform and for how much.
  • The onboarding was seen as clear and helpful to understand the changes. The spotlights made it easier to identify options, although some images felt repetitive or distracting.

Contribution: the findings helped consolidate the hierarchy between operations and products, support the tabs navigation structure and refine the selected proposal. They also guided improvements to the onboarding and to balance visibility.

Design iteration

From findings to a clearer, user-aligned proposal

07 · Design iteration

Objective: translate the findings of the first phases into a clearer proposal aligned with users' needs.

Aspects worked on

  • Onboarding refinement: improve the clarity of the orientation resources to help users understand the changes and find their way around the new Home.
  • Access to key information before operating: explore showing the balance from the first screen of the flow, so users can check what they need before continuing with an operation.

Contribution: the learnings were built into the proposal's evolution and made it possible to define which aspects needed further validation before going to production.

08 · Unmoderated quantitative validation: Maze

Objective: check whether users could find the products in the second tab of the new Home, and evaluate how much the onboarding contributed to that task in the iterated version.

Method: unmoderated test in Maze, comparing one experience with the onboarding and one without it.

What I evaluated

  • Whether participants managed to reach the products section.
  • Success in the discovery task.
  • Time needed to complete the task in each condition.

Participants who saw the onboarding were more successful and needed less time to find the products.

Contribution: prioritizing the onboarding for the first release to production, to make the new navigation and product discovery easier to understand.

Key findings

  • Everyday operations had to be the priority. Payments and frequent operations stood out among users' main needs.
  • Navigation shaped discovery. Products were easier to find through tabs than from the navigation bar that was evaluated.
  • Guidance eased the transition. The Maze results favored adding an onboarding to help people discover the Home's new structure.

My role

My role as UX Researcher

I worked with another researcher on planning and running the research, analyzing results and communicating findings to the Digital Product team and the Head of Digital Channels.

  • Designed the research plan and chose methods for each phase's objectives, aligned with stakeholders.
  • Designed the moderation guide and facilitated the qualitative study (usability + in-depth interviews).
  • Defined and set up the Qualtrics questionnaire for the quantitative survey.
  • Designed the protocol and moderated the guerrilla sessions in branches.
  • Followed up on, analyzed and interpreted findings across the different stages.
  • Synthesized and communicated insights to product and design teams to inform design decisions.
  • Collaborated actively in evaluating and adjusting design proposals based on the findings.

Understanding different saving habits to guide the evolution of a financial feature

Previous version of the experience, shown as historical documentation. Sensitive data is blurred.

Team

Scope
Saving behaviors, user expectations and the evolution of the experience
Methods
Behavioral analysis, qualitative research, 2 in-person workshops and thematic analysis

Alcancía was a feature of the Interbank app that let people set money aside from their available balance and keep it reserved. The research set out to understand the different habits, motivations and expectations of the people who used it, in order to find opportunities that could guide how the experience evolved.

The starting point

From data to hypotheses

Analyzing behavioral indicators made it possible to identify different patterns in the way people used the feature. While some users made frequent movements of money in and out, others kept their funds reserved for longer periods.

These differences led to hypotheses about the different purposes Alcancía could serve and about the experience needs associated with each behavior.

Pattern 1

Everyday money management

  • Frequent movements of money in and out.
  • Money set aside to organize expenses and recurring payments.
  • A need to keep flexibility and availability.

Pattern 2

Goal-oriented saving

  • Money reserved for longer.
  • Fewer withdrawals.
  • Saving linked to personal goals, in the medium or long term.

Conceptual illustration of the two patterns. It does not represent proportions of users. These patterns were a first approximation based on behavioral data, not final conclusions about people's motivations.

The data showed how people used the feature, but did not fully explain why they did.

Hypotheses that guided the study

H1

The feature could be serving different purposes depending on each person's financial habits.

H2

The flexibility to withdraw money could be valued by some users, but be a difficulty for those trying to keep their savings.

H3

Setting money apart from the available balance could create a greater sense of organization and control.

H4

Personal goals could influence the way people organize and keep their reserved money.

These were initial hypotheses to explore and test through qualitative research. They are not findings.

Objective and methodological approach

Understanding the “why” behind the behavior

Research objective

To understand how people used Alcancía, what they valued about the current experience, and what needs and expectations they had about its evolution.

Specific objectives

  1. Understand how people perceived the current experience.
  2. Explore saving habits and how people organize their money.
  3. Identify the motivations behind the different usage patterns.
  4. Identify needs and opportunities for the experience to evolve.

Why in-person workshops

As a methodological choice, workshops made it possible to bring together four things that matter when behavior needs explaining:

  • Exploring personal experiences
  • Contrasting perspectives
  • Encouraging discussion
  • Going deeper into the motivations behind observed behaviors

Research design and recruitment

Three behavior profiles, twelve participants

12participants
2in-person workshops
3behavior profiles
External agencycoordinated the recruitment

Transactional profile

People who made frequent movements of money in and out, using the feature mainly to organize expenses and payments.

Aspirational profile

People who used the feature to accumulate money and keep it reserved for long periods.

Mixed profile

People who combined movements in and out with an intention to keep money reserved over time.

The profiles were built from usage patterns observed over a three-month analysis period. The four participants per profile reflect the design of the research sample, not the composition of the product's user base.

Analysis and synthesis

From observed behavior to understood motivations

I led the thematic analysis of the sessions, identifying recurring patterns in participants' experiences, motivations and expectations.

Dimensions analyzed

  • Purpose of the money
  • Flexibility and availability
  • Sense of control and security
  • Organizing savings
  • Personal goals

What emerged

Four findings that connect behavior with meaning.

Explanatory visualization of the synthesis process. It is not an original artifact of the study.

Connecting the behaviors observed at the start with what we learned by talking to people is what turned a description of use into an understanding of purpose.

Findings

Four findings about what the feature meant to people

01

Related to hypothesis 1

Alcancía served different functions depending on the purpose of the money.

Some users valued having their money available when it was time to pay. Others valued that it stayed set aside.

Design opportunity: support different saving behaviors without assuming that everyone uses the feature for the same purpose.

02

Related to hypothesis 2

Flexibility makes saving easier, but also withdrawing.

Participants valued deciding how much to put in and when. But that same ease could make it harder to keep savings for a specific goal.

Design opportunity: explore how to balance flexibility with more control over withdrawals.

03

Related to hypothesis 3

Setting money apart creates a sense of organization, control and security.

Keeping money apart from the balance used day to day helped participants feel more in control of their spending. Some also saw it as a way to keep that money protected.

Design opportunity: strengthen the role of the feature as a space to organize and protect money according to its intended use.

04

Related to hypothesis 4

Saving for goals doesn't necessarily mean looking for returns.

For people who saved toward personal goals, organizing money around a purpose could matter more than what it earned.

Design opportunity: make it easier to structure savings around individual goals and preferences.

From findings to design opportunities

One central tension

Flexibility

Letting people set money aside when they need to and have it available for their expenses.

⇄
Protecting the purpose

Helping keep reserved the money meant for savings goals.

The opportunity was to explore how to balance both without removing the autonomy that made the feature useful. The findings fed collaborative conversations and explorations with Product Designers.

The thread of the case

  1. HypothesesPatterns in behavioral data raised questions about purpose.
  2. ResearchTwo workshops, 12 participants, three profiles.
  3. FindingsWhat the feature meant for each person.
  4. OpportunitiesBalancing flexibility and protecting the purpose.

My role

My role as UX Researcher

What I led

  • Defining the research objectives and participant selection criteria.
  • Designing and refining the workshop guide.
  • Preparing the materials.
  • Coordinating the recruitment.
  • Facilitating the sessions.
  • Leading the thematic analysis.
  • Presenting the findings to leadership.
  • Turning the findings into recommendations.

Where I collaborated

  • Working with Product Designers to explore and evaluate solutions.

I did not design the interfaces or prototypes: my responsibility was the research and bringing its evidence into those conversations.

Closing and reflection

The research made it possible to understand that a single feature can respond to very different financial needs. By connecting behavioral patterns with people's experiences and motivations, we were able to identify opportunities to guide the evolution of the product from a deeper understanding of its users.

What I took from it

One of the main lessons of this project was understanding that research is not only about identifying which features people need, but about understanding what a tool means to them and what role it plays in their everyday life.
